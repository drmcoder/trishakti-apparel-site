Buyer Targeting & Growth Brief

Built from two Pakistan export-data pulls (sweatshirts/hoodies and sportswear, May 2026), verified against current tariff schedules. It corrects two things our website and existing buyer list currently get wrong.

The three findings that change what we do

  1. Our duty-free pitch does not work against most competitors. Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Cambodia — and India since 15 July 2026 — all enter the UK at 0%. Our duty edge is real only against China and Indonesia, and against India into the EU. Lead with duty only when the buyer currently sources from China.
  2. Our published MOQ locks us out of our best-fit market. Around 80% of sportswear shipments are under 500 pcs — the least price-sensitive and least audit-gated segment. Our site says 500–1,000; our own positioning claims 300–500.
  3. Every bulk and retail-chain buyer requires a social audit we don't have. Sedex membership is paid, but the SAQ sits at 0% and no SMETA is booked. This gates the entire volume strategy — importers, chains and agents alike.

3 · The prices in the report are not a benchmark

  • Rows tagged EFS SRO 957 are under Pakistan's Export Facilitation Scheme — inputs imported under bond. Where fabric is buyer-supplied, declared value is the making charge only.
  • The UNIT column mixes per-kg and per-piece. Mango's "$13.34" is per kilo (~$3/pc).
  • Single POs are split across several bills of lading, and 15-piece rows are development samples.
Sanity check

Woolworth: 9,018 pcs, $19,824 = $2.20/pc for a printed 41/59 hooded sweatshirt. A hoodie takes 0.6–0.8 kg of fleece; even at $3/kg that's ~$2 of fabric before a stitch. That is a CMT charge or a data error — not a garment price. We cannot make it and should not try.

Only trust rows where Incoterm, fabric inclusion and spec are known. In this dataset that is PL Schoolwear ($5.10–5.80) and Vixn Fit ($13.37) — both per piece, both UK. Price our own work bottom-up: fabric consumption × fabric price + trims + CMT + overhead + margin.

4 · MOQ — the constraint to solve

100 machines will happily run 300 pieces. The binding constraint is almost certainly fabric — Chinese mills typically hold 300–500 kg minimums per colour. Confirm which it is, then choose:

  • Hold core fabric in 4–6 staple weights and colours so small orders cut from stock — ties up cash, but turns MOQ into a real weapon.
  • Buy from a stockist rather than a mill for small runs.
  • ★ Offer a 300-pc trial tier at +20–25%. Buyers accept a premium on a first order; nobody hands a 5,000-pc first order to an unaudited factory they have never met. The trial converts them — the reorder is the business.

C · Retail chains and department stores — highest volume, hardest gate

Ahlens (SE), Woolworth (DE), Mango (ES), Inditex (ES), Fashion Nova (US). Understand how they actually buy before targeting them:

  • The audit is pre-qualifying. BSCI, SMETA or their own code. No audit, no purchase order.
  • They rarely onboard factories directly. Most source through buying agents and sourcing offices. One agent relationship routes chain orders — that is the realistic door, not a cold email to head office.
  • They buy to a calendar, 6–9 months ahead. To land AW27 we must be in front of buyers by early 2027 — which is exactly what the January shows are for.
  • Vendor onboarding needs a DUNS number (free), bank details, a factory profile and usually a physical audit visit.
  • Tier realistically. Inditex, Mango and H&M are not a 12-month target. Regional department stores, discount chains, supermarket clothing lines and catalogue/online retailers are.

D · Deprioritise

Individual US buyers ordering 1–100 pcs of team kit — below MOQ, no duty advantage. And the sub-300 custom-jersey segment runs on dye-sublimation printing, a capability we don't have; those Sialkot and Faisalabad sellers are purpose-built for it.

7 · Vetting a buyer

Outbound is self-verifying — when we pick a real brand, legitimacy is largely settled. Fraud arrives through inbound marketplace leads.

Ten-minute free checks: EU VIES (VAT number must match the name) · UK Companies House · DE Handelsregister / NL KvK / DK CVR / SE Bolagsverket · the person's LinkedIn history · domain age and a company-domain email, not Gmail · can we find their product in real shops?

Payment structure matters more than vetting:

  • 30% advance TT, 70% against copy of B/L
  • Never release the original B/L before payment — whoever holds it controls the cargo
  • L/C only when advised through our own Nepali bank. Never accept a PDF from the buyer — forged L/Cs are the most common large-value export fraud and look perfect to an untrained eye
  • Never ship open account or DA terms to a new buyer
  • Charge for samples and freight — this filters more time-wasters than any other rule
Walk away if

They ask us to pay any fee (registration, agent, certification, customs — always a scam) · a huge first order with no spec questions and no price negotiation · a bank in a different country from the company · a sudden bank-detail change mid-deal (confirm by phone on a number we already had) · they ask for an under-declared invoice.

On under-invoicing: it is customs fraud in the buyer's country and a foreign-exchange offence here. Export proceeds are reconciled against the export declaration, so a $2-declared / $9-received mismatch is exactly what that system detects. It risks the origin privilege the whole business depends on, kills L/C access, and — decisively — saves nothing, because most target buyers already pay 0% duty. A buyer who wants a false invoice will also dispute quality to avoid the balance payment.
